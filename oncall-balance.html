01
The Problem

Burnout that never shows up in a Jira ticket.

On-call engineers get paged at 2 AM, absorb post-incident stress, lose sleep, and none of it gets tracked anywhere. The 2025 SRE Report, published by Catchpoint, based on a survey of 301 engineers, confirmed what everyone already felt.

🌙

On-call pages fire at 2 AM. Sleep gets cut short. There's no recovery window built into the schedule. The next shift starts regardless.

📉

14% of engineers report higher stress post-incident. Support available to them drops from 55% to 44% the moment the incident closes. (SRE Report 2025)

🔒

The engineers who need help most won't accept employer surveillance of their health. Any solution that touches HR data is dead on arrival.

🔁

Existing company mental health resources require the engineer to self-identify and reach out. Nobody in crisis does that. Detection has to be proactive.

02
Research & Process

Research before a single line of code.

We grounded everything in real SRE data and validated the UX in FigJam before touching Flutter. Not because it was required, because we needed to know we were solving the right problem.

01

SRE stress journey mapping

Started with the 2025 SRE Report (Catchpoint, n=301) and mapped the full engineer stress arc, from on-call assignment through incident, resolution, and the invisible recovery gap that follows.

02

Data pairing validation

Validated that Apple Watch sleep signals and Rootly workload data could be meaningfully correlated. Found that Rootly's open-source On-Call Health model already had research-backed weights we could build on.

03

FigJam ideation: team of 3

Ran collaborative ideation in FigJam before any prototyping. The key architectural decision made here: separate the risk model from the AI layer entirely. Deterministic scoring first, language second.

04

Figma prototyping: all 4 screens

Wireframed Dashboard, Trends, Resources, and Support before writing Flutter. This made the build window clean, no design decisions mid-coding, no wasted time.

The Product

Four screens. One purpose.

Dashboard, Trends, Resources, Support. Every screen designed around what an on-call engineer actually needs, not what looks impressive in a pitch deck.

01

Dashboard

Heart rate, HRV, sleep duration, incident severity, on-call status, all in one view. The AI-generated daily insight ties your actual numbers into one plain-English sentence.

02

Trends

On-call spike detection, mid-week drift patterns, context switching cost analysis. Shows you what's building before you feel it.

03

Resources

Mindfulness, breathing, confidential counseling links. In high-risk states, Crisis Services Canada surfaces directly, not buried in a menu, not replaced by a breathing exercise.

04

Support

Anonymous peer matching, verified mentor profiles. Zero biometric data shared. Opt-in only, nothing fires automatically.

Ethics & Privacy

The commitments we made before writing any code.

01

What we do

  • Process all data on-device only
  • Fully opt-in, user controls everything
  • Evidence-based resource suggestions
  • Surface real human support in crisis states, not just breathing tips
  • Make every AI insight explainable
  • PHIPA, PIPEDA and HIPAA-compliant architecture
02

What we don't do

  • Share data with employers or third parties
  • Diagnose medical or mental health conditions
  • Replace therapists or professional care
  • Auto-escalate without user consent
  • Track without the user's full awareness
  • Make decisions on behalf of the engineer
03

How we mitigated risk

  • False positives: context-aware thresholds
  • Over-reliance: prompt professional referrals
  • Stigma: private-first, no sharing defaults
  • Data gaps: graceful degradation
  • Crisis states: immediate human resources surfaced
  • Misinterpretation: every insight explained
07
What I Learned

What this project taught me.

01

Separate the AI from the judgment

Letting Claude compute the risk score would make the system a black box. Using a deterministic model first, then having Claude translate it into language, made everything explainable, testable, and defensible, especially in a health context.

02

Ethics is a design constraint, not an afterthought

Building in a mental health space forces you to think about what happens when someone is vulnerable and relying on your output. Every feature decision had to pass a "what if they're actually in crisis" test. That thinking made the product stronger.

03

Research before code is always worth it

Spending the first hour and a half on FigJam instead of Flutter felt risky in an 8-hour hackathon. It paid off. We built the right thing cleanly, instead of building fast and course-correcting the whole way through.
